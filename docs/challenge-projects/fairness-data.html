Challenge Project: Fairness Data

your questions, your figures, your story

In the Guided Projects you learned one part of the grammar of graphics at a time, on both of your datasets. In this Challenge Project you choose and combine those tools to answer two or three questions of your own about one dataset, your Fairness Data, and you publish a report and slides that tell what you found. You’ll present one of your two Challenge Projects on Friday, December 4.

AI use: Level 2 (AI Collaboration). See “AI use” in Reference. Due: see Blackboard for the due date and time.

Learning outcomes

This project assesses the course learning outcomes (syllabus). You will:

  • Assess the accuracy and honesty of data visualizations by applying principles of graphical integrity.
  • Design original data graphics that maximize data density and minimize chartjunk, using principles such as data-ink maximization and multifunctional graphical elements.
  • Analyze how visual design choices (e.g., scale, proportion, labeling, and layout) influence the interpretation of quantitative information.
  • Identify and define the core components of the grammar of graphics, including data, aesthetics, geoms, stats, scales, coordinates, and facets.
  • Construct visualizations using layered grammar and describe the role of each component while building a plot.
  • Apply appropriate geoms and aesthetics from the grammar of graphics to explore relationships in a dataset.
  • Compare and contrast different visualizations (obtained with the grammar of graphics) to evaluate how changes in aesthetics, geoms, or scales affect the interpretation of data.
  • Design and implement custom visualizations using the grammar of graphics, incorporating advanced features such as faceting, themes, and coordinate transformations to effectively communicate insights from data.

Do the project in six tasks

Each task: the code goes in the script, your words go in the report (and the slides). Render and push whenever you stop. Unlike the Guided Projects, the script has no PASS / TO DO checks for each task: the checklist in Finish and submit is your check.

Task 1 · Your questions

Goal: Two or three questions of your own that tell one story.

Step What you do How it’s checked
Questions Write two or three questions, one sentence each, naming the outcome and the groups compared. They connect: for example, an overall pattern, then who it differs for, then whether it holds over time or within subgroups Your instructor approves them in the proposal; the final must match or explain changes
Report Put your questions at the top of the report and on your Question slide Your instructor reads them (counts under Questions and data)

Two questions make a complete, full-credit project. More questions or more slides don’t earn more credit.

Example (penguins, not your data):

# Question Outcome Groups compared
1 Do the three penguin species differ in body mass? body mass (g) species
2 Does the species difference hold for both sexes? body mass (g) species × sex

Question patterns that work: a rate within groups defined by a sensitive attribute (who has the outcome more often?); an overall pattern, then a subgroup; before and after an event, a season or a year (“compared to what?”, I6); the same comparison in panels.

Your project is yours

Several of you work with the same dataset. That’s fine, but your Challenge Project must be your own and substantially different from every other student’s, including classmates who use the same data.

  • Your questions are independent. No question may match another student’s: the same outcome compared across the same groups counts as a match, even if it’s worded differently.
  • Your figures and writing are your own. You may talk about ideas with classmates. Write your own code, make your own figures, and write your own findings, limits and “why it matters.”
  • Say where help came from. If a classmate, a tutor or an AI tool shaped your work, say so: in your AI log for AI tools, and in a one-line acknowledgment in your report for people.
  • Be ready to explain it. You should be able to explain any figure or line of code in your project, on December 4 or if I ask before then.

Projects that are copies of each other (same questions, same figures) don’t meet this requirement, and copying falls under the Catawba College Honor Code.

Task 2 · Prepare your data for your questions

Goal: Make the groups, years or subsets your questions need, and show what each step did.

Step What you do How it’s checked
Script One section per data move: mutate() a year or a group, filter(), or lumping groups with care. Print the rows and the groups (with n) before and after each move Your instructor reads the code and output (counts under Questions and data)
Report For each move, one line: what it does and why your question needs it. In your Data paragraph and slide: where the data come from, what one row is, how many rows, and what you made or filtered Your instructor reads it

Never drop or combine a group silently: say what you removed or lumped, and how many rows that was.

Example:

Move Code Why my question needs it Before → after
Drop penguins with no sex recorded filter(!is.na(sex)) Question 2 compares sexes 344 → 333 rows (11 removed; said in the report)
Make a period (gapminder) mutate(period = if_else(year < 1980, "before 1980", "1980 on")) A before-and-after comparison 1,704 rows; 12 years → 2 periods, 852 rows each

Task 3 · Figures that answer your questions

Goal: At least one figure for each question, each one making its finding easy to see.

Step What you do How it’s checked
Script Make each figure in its own chunk (## ---- fig1 ----, fig2, …). Across the project: at least one figure with facets, and at least one with a deliberate scale or coordinate choice (log or percent scale, zoom with coord_cartesian(), flipped axes) Your instructor reads the code and figures (counts under Figures and grammar of graphics choices)
Report Under each figure: a headline that states the finding; a grammar line (below); one or two sentences on what the figure shows Your instructor reads it

For every figure:

  • Headline states the finding (P2): “Gentoo penguins are the heaviest species”, not “Body mass by species”.
  • Grammar line: name the figure’s components and their roles. Example: “data: one row per penguin; aesthetics: species → x, body mass → y, sex → panels; geom: points with a mean marker; scale: y in grams, fixed across panels; facet: sex.”
  • Alt text (fig-alt): one sentence that says what the reader should see.
  • n for every group, in labels, strips or the subtitle.
  • Colors: run color_check() (at least 20 in all three rows), and color is never the only cue (D4).
  • Comparing groups defined by a sensitive attribute (sex, race, age band): show each group’s n, and use rates within groups rather than counts (I4). If a group is small, keep it visible and say so; don’t drop or lump it without saying why.
  • No private person’s name in any figure, table or slide.

Task 5 · Choose your path

Goal: Go further in one of two ways. Both earn the same credit: what counts is how well your figures answer your questions, not which path you take.

Path A · Go deeper with the tools you know. Combine what you learned in GP11–GP13 more ambitiously than any single Guided Project task did. For example:

  • layer stats, scales and facets in one figure (rates with error bars, in panels, on a percent axis);
  • show uncertainty for every group comparison (error bars or resampled clouds);
  • annotate important events or values on the figure (I2);
  • order groups so the finding reads first;
  • replace several separate plots with one set of small multiples that share an axis.

Path B · Bring in one new R tool. Learn one package on your own and use it for at least one figure. Your skills carry over: most tools still map data to aesthetics, draw geoms and use scales. Examples: patchwork (combine figures into one layout), ggrepel (direct labels that don’t overlap), ggridges (distributions for many groups), plotly or ggiraph (interactive figures on your report page).

If you choose Path B:

  • Explain the mapping: add a short section to your report, “How my new tool fits the grammar of graphics”: which aesthetics, geoms, stats, scales or facets it uses or replaces.
  • Keep the course rules: %>%, color_check() passes, color is never the only cue, alt text on every figure.
  • It must work where your project lives: it installs in Posit Cloud, and your report and slides still render and publish.
  • Cite it in your Sources, and log any AI help with it.
  • If it doesn’t work by the deadline, go back to Path A for that figure and say so in one line. That isn’t a penalty.

Say in your proposal which path you’ll take (and for Path B, which tool and which figure).

The 14 codes (Module 14)

Integrity: I1 Size matches the numbers (Lie Factor) · I2 Label clearly; annotate important events · I3 Show data variation, not design variation · I4 Standardized units (rates, per capita) · I5 No more dimensions than the data · I6 Compared to what?

Data-ink: D1 Always plot the data · D2 Mute the grid; data ink first · D3 Label directly, not only through a legend · D4 Color is never the only cue

Presenting: P1 Story arc: question, data, finding, limits, why it matters · P2 The headline states the finding · P3 One message per slide · P4 Explain a figure aloud: axes, where to look, what it means

Privacy

Your data describe people by sensitive attributes (sex, race, age). Describe groups respectfully, show small groups with their n, and say “differences, not causes.”

Grading (percentage ranges in the rubric)

Levels (all criteria): Exemplary 90–100% · Proficient 80–89% · Developing 65–79% · Beginning 1–64% · No Response 0%.

Criterion Weight Looks at
Questions and data 15% Two or three independent questions naming outcome and groups; data moves with why and n before and after; matches your approved proposal, or changes explained
Figures and grammar of graphics choices 25% Each question answered by a figure; components chosen for a reason; grammar lines; facets and a scale or coordinate choice; Path A depth or Path B mapping
Integrity and data-ink 20% Integrity audit with one before-and-after; group comparisons with n and rates; color check, never color alone; specific alt text; no private names
Story and interpretation 20% Question, data, finding, limits, why it matters; headlines state findings; differences, not causes; small groups named; slides that show findings
Code and publishing 10% One script feeds the report and the slides; both render and are published; no template text left
AI disclosure 10% AI use line in your report and slides, AI log typed in Blackboard (or the no-AI statement), attestation, and copies of all AI conversations uploaded

An idea of your own that helps the reader see the finding (an angle, a comparison, an order, an annotation) is what separates Exemplary from Proficient. Decoration doesn’t count; an idea that distorts the data or breaks the color rules earns no credit.
